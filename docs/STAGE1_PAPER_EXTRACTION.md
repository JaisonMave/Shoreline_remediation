## Paper 1: Challenger, Gmur & Taylor (2021)

### Citation and access
- Citation: Challenger, G.E., Gmur, S., Taylor, E. (2021). A review of Gulf of Mexico coastal marsh erosion studies following the 2010 Deepwater Horizon oil spill and comparison to over 4 years of shoreline loss data from Fall 2010 to Summer 2015. *Marine Pollution Bulletin* 164, 111983. **VERIFIED** (Europe PMC record: volume 164, article/page 111983, print date 2021-03; OpenAlex: vol 164, published online 2021-01-26).
- DOI: 10.1016/j.marpolbul.2021.111983. **VERIFIED** (PubMed/Europe PMC record; DOI resolves to Elsevier PII S0025326X21000175). PMID 33513545.
- It is a journal article in *Marine Pollution Bulletin*, **not** an IOSC proceedings paper. I found no IOSC 2021 version. **NOT FOUND** (IOSC version).
- Keywords: Erosion; Spartina; Gulf of Mexico; Deepwater Horizon; Oiling. **VERIFIED** (Europe PMC).
- Version read: **abstract only** plus secondary description.

### Study area and sites
- Spill affected nearly 1105 km of coastal marsh (abstract). **VERIFIED**
- Shoreline retreat measurements from **multiple previous DWH studies** were combined for **131 herbaceous marsh sample sites**. **VERIFIED** (abstract)
- Geographic scale: Zengel et al. 2022 Table 1 classes it as "Large (LA)" = wider Louisiana. **SECONDARY** (Zengel et al. 2022, *Sci Rep* 12:13802, Table 1).
- Which underlying studies were pooled: **NOT FOUND.** Abstract says only "multiple studies". Zengel et al. 2022 Table 1 classes Challenger's design as "Field (CI)" (field-based, control-impact). That suggests the pooled data were field shoreline-retreat measurements, but the specific source studies are not named in anything I could read.
- Site type: "herbaceous marsh" (abstract). Keyword "Spartina". **VERIFIED**

### Time periods
- Fall 2010 to Summer 2015 overall ("over 4 years"). **VERIFIED** (title, abstract)
- At least one sub-interval is Fall 2010 to Fall 2011 (the only interval with a significant increase). **VERIFIED** (abstract)
- Other sub-interval boundaries: **NOT FOUND.**

### Oiling definition
- Abstract uses the term "heavily oiled shorelines". **VERIFIED**
- Zengel et al. 2022 Table 1 lists the oiling levels with an erosion effect as "Heavy to very heavy (treated)". **SECONDARY**
- Whether SCAT was used, which SCAT product (maximum or survey-specific), field oil measurements, and the comparison group: **NOT FOUND.**
- Note on wording: the abstract says the significant increase was for heavily oiled shorelines **"relative to other periods"**. So, as the abstract words it, the reported contrast is between time periods within heavily oiled shoreline, **not** heavily oiled vs unoiled. Whether an oiled-vs-reference contrast was also tested: **NOT FOUND.**

### Erosion measurement
- The reviewed literature used ground sampling and/or aerial/satellite imagery (abstract). **VERIFIED**
- The 131-site analysis used "shoreline retreat measurements" (abstract). **VERIFIED**
- Method (stakes/GPS/imagery), resolution and units: **NOT FOUND.** (Zengel 2022 Table 1 labels it "Field (CI)": **SECONDARY**.)

### Exposure, pre-spill rates, cleanup, hurricanes
- Zengel et al. 2022 (abstract and Introduction, p. 1) says Challenger et al. and Deis et al. questioned most earlier DWH erosion studies. Their grounds were possible design flaws and confounding, mainly tropical cyclones and different wave exposure at oiled vs reference sites. **SECONDARY**
- Zengel et al. 2022 (p. 5, Hurricane Isaac section) says some papers argued that Hurricane Isaac alone may have caused erosion that was wrongly attributed to oil. It cites refs 18 and 19, i.e. Deis et al. and Challenger et al. **SECONDARY.** How Challenger et al. treated Isaac in their own analysis: **NOT FOUND.**
- Cleanup treatment: Zengel 2022 Table 1 gives the cleanup effect as "Undetermined". The footnote defines this as including treated marsh without direct comparison to untreated sites at similar oiling, or with inconclusive results. **SECONDARY**
- How Challenger handled wave exposure or pre-spill erosion rates in their own analysis: **NOT FOUND.**

### Statistics and effect sizes
- Significant increase in shoreline loss **only** for Fall 2010 to Fall 2011, heavily oiled shorelines, relative to other periods. **VERIFIED** (abstract). No magnitude in abstract.
- Zengel 2022 Table 1 gives Challenger's DWH erosion effect as "Yes (97–107%)". The table footnote defines this as the percent increase in linear erosion rate. **SECONDARY.** I could not confirm which comparison or periods this percentage refers to.
- Statistical test, m/yr rates by oiling class and period, n per class, uncertainty: **NOT FOUND.**

### Duration of oil effect
- Abstract: significant only Fall 2010 to Fall 2011; "The evidence does not suggest widespread long-term coastal marsh erosion from the DWH." **VERIFIED** (the quote is under 12 words)
- Zengel 2022 Table 1, "Duration of DWH erosion effect" for Challenger: "1 year (heavy), 3–4 years (very heavy, treated)". **SECONDARY.** This implies Challenger reported a longer effect in a very-heavy/treated subset. I could not check this against the paper; the abstract does not mention it.

### Island loss / site survivorship
- **NOT FOUND.**

### Limitations stated
- The abstract says the paper "discusses their limitations and sometimes conflicting results", referring to previous studies. **VERIFIED.** Challenger's own stated limitations: **NOT FOUND.**

### Data availability
- **NOT FOUND.** I found no deposited dataset for the 131-site compilation.

### Later critique (context only, SECONDARY)
- Zengel et al. 2022 *Sci Rep* responded directly to the concerns raised by Challenger/Deis. They used a BACI design and found that excluding post-Isaac imagery gave results indistinguishable from the original. They modelled wave power and found small differences: mean about 3 vs 7 W/m for reference vs oiled sites. They concluded that erosion increased 112–233% for 2 years in heavily oiled marsh. These are Zengel's own results, not Challenger's.
- Zengel et al. 2022 *Ecol Appl* 32:e02489 (p. 12) cites Challenger et al. 2021 for "contrasting results and interpretations". It also lists Challenger among studies reporting accelerated erosion for one to three years. **SECONDARY**

## Paper 2: Turner, McClenachan & Tweel (2016)

### Citation and access
- Citation: Turner, R.E., McClenachan, G., Tweel, A.W. (2016). Islands in the oil: Quantifying salt marsh shoreline erosion after the Deepwater Horizon oiling. *Marine Pollution Bulletin* 110(1), 316–323. **VERIFIED** (Europe PMC, OpenAlex, LSU repository record).
- DOI: 10.1016/j.marpolbul.2016.06.046. **VERIFIED.** PMID 27349381. Elsevier PII S0025326X16304507. Open access (hybrid, CC BY-NC-ND) per OpenAlex/Semantic Scholar, but I could not retrieve it.
- Version read: **abstract only**, plus the authors' GRIIDC dataset (opened), plus secondary descriptions.

### Study area and sites
- **46 islands**, measured before and after the spill (abstract). **VERIFIED**
- Estuaries: the GRIIDC dataset is titled "Erosion Rates of Islands in Barataria Bay, Breton Sound, and Terrebonne Bay, 1988-2013" (UDI R4.x264.196:0008, author R.E. Turner). So the sites are **not only Barataria Bay**. **VERIFIED in dataset metadata**; the paper's own study-area wording: **NOT FOUND.**
- From the data file (Sheet2, "Length (north to south)"), 46 site rows. These are my own counts from the file, not numbers reported in the paper:
  - East Barataria Bay: 16 oiled, 4 unoiled.
  - West Barataria Bay: 2 oiled, 11 unoiled.
  - Terrebonne Bay: 4 oiled, 6 unoiled.
  - Breton Sound: 0 oiled, 3 unoiled.
  - Total: **22 oiled, 24 unoiled.**
  - Sheet1 ("Width (east to west)") has 41 site rows: 20 oiled, 21 unoiled.
- Site type: salt-marsh islands (title). The authors describe the islands as "sentinels of marsh stability" (abstract). **VERIFIED**

### Time periods
- Imagery 1989–2012 (GRI news release; EOM story). **SECONDARY.** The data file holds widths/lengths at "1988/8 [Sept 1990]" and "May 2010", and the GRIIDC data-collection period is 1988-01-01 to 2012-12-31. **VERIFIED in dataset**
- Erosion-rate columns in the data file (all in m y-1). **VERIFIED in dataset**; how the paper labels them: **NOT FOUND.** Header wording differs slightly between sheets.
  1. Linear regression before spill (with n photos and R²).
  2. "erosion before 2010".
  3. "After spill and in 2010".
  4. "April to April, first 12 mo after spill".
  5. "First 4-8 months in 2011".
  6. "erosion Spring 2011 to Dec 2012" (Sheet1 says "May 2011 to Dec 2012").
  7. "erosion May 2010 to Dec 2012".
- GRIIDC abstract: rates calculated for five intervals, from the initial post-oiling period in 2010 to the end of 2012. **VERIFIED in dataset metadata**
- Abstract intervals: "first six months after the oiling" and "first 2.5 years after the oiling". **VERIFIED**

### Oiling definition
- The data file gives each site a **SCAT colour category**: red (heavy), orange (moderate), yellow (light), green (very light), light green (trace), blue (no oil). Many sites carry mixed codes (e.g. "R G Y", "Or Y", "bl yel"). It also gives a binary code: 1 = unoiled, 2 = oiled. **VERIFIED in dataset/metadata**
- So the comparison appears to be binary: oiled (any SCAT oiling, light to heavy) vs unoiled (blue). Zengel et al. 2022 Table 1 describes Turner's oiling levels as "Oiled (light–heavy)". **SECONDARY.** One example of a mixed code: site 1.07 East Barataria is coded "bl yel" and assigned category 2 (oiled). **VERIFIED in dataset**
- Which SCAT product was used (maximum oiling, survey-specific, date): **NOT FOUND.** Field oil measurements: **NOT FOUND** (none in the data file). There is no separate "heavily oiled" class in the binary analysis, as far as the data file shows.

### Erosion measurement
- Island width (east–west) and length (north–south) measured in **Google Earth** historical imagery. Each island had a distinctive geomorphic feature as reference point (GRI release; GRIIDC methods "Google Earth measurements"). **SECONDARY / dataset metadata**
- Units: m and m y-1. **VERIFIED in dataset**
- Precision check (data file, Sheet3): repeated measurements of distances between fixed structures at 6 locations. The SEM ranged 0.08–0.33 m and the coefficient of variation was ≤0.009 (the column is labelled "COV (%)", values 0.0014–0.0089). **VERIFIED in dataset.** Image resolution and provider: **NOT FOUND.**

### Exposure, pre-spill rates, cleanup, hurricanes
- Pre-spill: before the spill, average width, length and erosion rate were similar for oiled and unoiled islands (GRI release; EOM). **SECONDARY.** The data file contains a pre-spill linear-regression rate per site. **VERIFIED in dataset**
- Wave exposure/fetch: **NOT FOUND.**
- Cleanup treatment: **NOT FOUND.** Zengel 2022 Table 1 shows "–" for the cleanup effect.
- Hurricane Isaac (Aug 2012): Zengel et al. 2022 (p. 5) states that "Turner et al. also found that Hurricane Isaac had little influence on salt marsh shoreline retreat in the region". **SECONDARY.** Not in the abstract, and not in the GRI release.

### Statistics and effect sizes
- Erosion rates at oiled islands "increased to 275%" in the first six months after oiling. **VERIFIED** (abstract wording). Note the ambiguity: the GRI release and EOM say "rose about 275%" / "increased 275%". "To 275%" (2.75×) and "by 275%" (3.75×) differ. The baseline (pre-spill rate of the same islands, or unoiled islands) is not stated in the abstract.
- Oiled-island rates were **200% of the unoiled islands** for the first 2.5 years after oiling. **VERIFIED** (abstract). That is about 2×, which GRI describes as "twice the rate".
- The oiled-island rate was **twelve times** the average deltaic-plain land loss of **0.4 % y⁻¹** (1988–2011). **VERIFIED** (abstract). Note the unit is percent per year (area-based), not m/yr.
- Zengel 2022 Table 1 gives Turner's effect as "Yes (224%)" increase in linear erosion rate, with duration 2.5 years. **SECONDARY.** This 224% figure does not appear in the abstract; I could not trace its derivation.
- Absolute m/yr means by group and period, SE/CI, n per group, statistical test and p-values: **NOT FOUND** (paper text not reached). Per-site rates are in the GRIIDC file. I have **not** computed group means, to avoid presenting my numbers as the paper's.
- Data-file observation, reported as a fact about the file and not interpreted: many East Barataria and Terrebonne sites have **blank** values for intervals 4–6 but do have a May 2010–Dec 2012 value. The file does not say why.

### Duration of oil effect / recovery
- The enhanced rate declined over about 2.5 years but stayed about twice the unoiled rate (GRI release). **SECONDARY**
- "No evidence of reversal in the aggregate", i.e. lost shoreline did not recover (GRI release headline: "no obvious recovery"). **SECONDARY**
- Abstract asked whether losses were temporary and whether recovery/restoration occurred. The answer is not explicit in the abstract. **VERIFIED** (question only)
- Mechanism: the authors suggest oiling compromised belowground biomass (abstract). **VERIFIED**

### Island loss / survivorship
- Islands described as sentinels of marsh stability already in decline before the spill (abstract). **VERIFIED**
- One bird rookery abandoned; other islands have shorter projected lifespans (GRI release, EOM, attributed to Turner). **SECONDARY**
- Quantified island disappearance or lifetime estimates: **NOT FOUND.** GRI says the authors anticipate such estimates but none are given there.

### Limitations stated
- **NOT FOUND** (paper text not reached).

### Data availability
- Public on GRIIDC: DOI 10.7266/N72J68WS, UDI R4.x264.196:0008. One zip (20.7 KB) containing "R4.x264.196.0008_data to Jason islands.2.xlsx", with Sheet1 width, Sheet2 length and Sheet3 precision. **VERIFIED** (downloaded via https://data.griidc.org/download/dataset/2486 and opened). The GRI release also says the data are in the paper's Supporting Online Materials. **SECONDARY**
